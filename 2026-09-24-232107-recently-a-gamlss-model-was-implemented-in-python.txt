  What is transferable to BrainAGE

  1. A likelihood-based inverse estimator as a complementary output. Fit a
  proper normative model per feature (GAMLSS or warped BLR, with sex and site
  as random effects), then estimate brain age as argmax_a Σ_i log p(y_i | a),
  ideally with a low-rank residual covariance. This gives:

  a calibration-type estimate that is unbiased conditional on age, without
  post-hoc correction;
  regional brain age maps as a by-product;
  uncertainty per subject.

  Because the inputs are CAT12 outputs, this can be built directly on CAT12 ROI
  features. It is also a natural fit for FoldingAGE, since gyrification
  trajectories are well suited to normative modelling. My expectation, which is
  an assumption and not a result, is that it would perform about equally to or
  worse than GPR-based BrainAGE once the downstream analysis is correctly
  adjusted for age. If so, that would itself be a publishable clarification of
  the paper's claims.

  Simulation: likelihood estimator vs linear-kernel GPR

  BA_gpr_core uses a linear kernel, and the default trend correction is linear.
  I simulated 2,500 training subjects, 60 correlated features with linear age
  trends, and patients shifted 4 years along the aging axis. The normative model
  was fitted with the actual _fit_location_scale.

  ┌────────────────────────────────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐
  │               Estimator                │    MAE in     │ Age-adjusted  │
  │                                        │   controls    │       d       │
  ├────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┤
  │ Ridge (≈ linear GPR) + linear trend    │ 1.74          │ 1.68          │
  │ correction                             │               │               │
  ├────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┤
  │ ML, features treated as independent    │ 2.52          │ 1.27          │
  ├────────────────────────────────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┤
  │ ML, full residual correlation          │ 1.79          │ 1.69          │
  └────────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────┘

  The two estimates correlate at r = 0.999 in controls. Two things follow:

  1. In the linear-Gaussian case they are the same estimator. Cov(y) = Σ + v·mmᵀ
     (Σ is the residual covariance, m the per-feature age slopes, v the
     variance of age). Sherman–Morrison therefore gives Cov(y)⁻¹m ∝ Σ⁻¹m. So the
     regression brain age and the calibration estimate are the same linear
     score, rescaled by R², and the trend correction removes that rescaling. Any
     advantage of NDM must come from the nonlinear μ(a), the age-dependent σ(a)
     or non-Gaussian tails, not from the calibration itself. This supports your
     expectation and gives the clarification paper a precise mechanism.
  2. The simple Σ_i log p over independent features does clearly worse. The
     low-rank covariance is necessary, not an optional refinement.

  Combining several normative models into one estimate

  Yes, in two ways.

  - Averaging point estimates. Each model's estimate is unbiased given true age,
    so any weighted average with weights summing to 1 stays unbiased. ensemble
    = 5 already normalizes its weights this way. You can therefore pass NDM
    predicted ages to ensemble_models as extra columns, even mixed with GPR
    models.
    - Optimal weights are w ∝ C⁻¹1, with C the covariance of the models' errors
      in controls. These differ from 1/MAE² when model errors are correlated, as
      for s4 and s8 of the same tissue.
  - Joint likelihood. Sum the log-likelihoods of all models and use one low-rank
    residual covariance across modalities. This amounts to one large normative
    model, and it yields a consistent per-subject uncertainty.

  A second simulation compared these with two models, e.g. GM and WM:

  ┌───────────────────────────────┬────────────────┐

  ├───────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤

  ├───────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤

  What it does:
  - Input: reads the BA_data2mat mat-files, both v5 and v7.3/HDF5, including
    surface ind. Files joined with + are concatenated and treated as separate
    sites, as in BrainAGE.
  - Normative model: the ComBatLS location-scale fit, copied into the file so
    BrainAGE doesn't depend on the ComCat repo. It uses a natural cubic spline
    of age for both μ and log σ, with sex and site as covariates.
  - Brain age: the age with the highest likelihood on a 0.25-year grid, refined
    with a parabola; the curvature of that parabola gives a per-subject SD.
  - Lobe maps: use the same atlas files and conventions as BA_gpr_ui (10 volume
    regions, 8 on the surface).
  - Ensemble: combines the models with weights that sum to 1: mean, mae (the
    same as D.ensemble = 5) or gls.
  - Test data: --test together with --adjust 1:108 adapts the models to the test
    site using its controls, and estimates the ensemble weights from those
    controls.
  - Comparison: a Python copy of the GPR BrainAGE with linear trend correction
    runs on the same folds.
  - Output: a .mat file (struct NDM, including fold indices) and a .csv.

  What I found along the way:
  1. Per-voxel models as proposed don't work well here. With a low-rank residual
     correlation the likelihood is overconfident: coverage was 0.25 and MAE
     4.7, against 4.46 for GPR. The residual correlation of smoothed voxels is
     spread over hundreds of dimensions; the top 50 components explain only 39%.
     The default is therefore normative models on 100 PCA scores per model and
     region, with the full correlation. Per-voxel mode is still available as
     --pca 0.

     correction. Estimates are pulled toward middle age: mean BrainAGE runs from
     +1.8 at ages 12–20 to −1.2 above 65. This happens because the GM
     trajectories are curved and the errors are about 6 years. Averaging models
     removes noise but not this shared bias, so r grows from −0.11 per model to
     −0.23 for the ensemble. This bears directly on the paper's claim.
  3. The GM models have a mean offset of about +1.1 years in cross-validation.
     Test subjects' scores along the trailing components have only about 0.56×
     the training variance, and the log σ(a) term then favors old ages. --adjust
     removes this for test data, but cross-validation has no controls to adapt
     to.

     0.8 instead of 0.95.

  │               │ models                                                  │

  Your framing has a theoretical basis. In the linear-Gaussian case, the NDM's
  brain age is exactly the linear-kernel GPR with linear trend correction; the
  first simulation showed this. So BrainAGE and normative modelling are two
  views of the same model: brain age projects a subject's normative deviation
  onto the aging direction. That is a clean clarification of what the NDM paper
  claims, and it matches your original expectation.

  The most promising new idea is to split each subject's deviation into two
  parts. A GPR can't do this. At the estimated age â, the NDM gives a second
  number:
  - Aging-like part: D²(chronological age) − D²(â). This is the deviation that
    an age shift explains, i.e. BrainAGE.
  - Non-aging part: D²(â), the atypicality left over after the best age shift.
    It follows a χ² distribution with K degrees of freedom in healthy subjects,
    and it has regional versions too.

  (D² is the Mahalanobis distance of the z-scores at a given age.) All tests so
  far used only controls, and SD_cal assumes that patients look like older
  controls. Where a disorder isn't aging-like, the second part is exactly what
  BrainAGE misses. Your Dropbox has ADNI (200 AD / 404 MCI / 231 CN) and the
  Jena schizophrenia sample, so this can be tested directly. I'd compare the
  effect sizes of GPR BrainAGE, NDM brain age, the non-aging part, and both
  combined.

     This is your original hypothesis, and we never tested it. If it keeps child
     sensitivity, you get the lifespan advantage without the NDM.

  - It disappears with development-only training: all models are at about +0.2
